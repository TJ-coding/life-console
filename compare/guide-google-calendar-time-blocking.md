# How to time block in Google Calendar

<p class="meta">Steps checked 27 November 2026 against Google's Calendar Help: <a href="https://support.google.com/calendar/answer/9901136" rel="nofollow noopener">tasks</a>, <a href="https://support.google.com/calendar/answer/11190973" rel="nofollow noopener">Focus time</a>, <a href="https://support.google.com/calendar/answer/7638168" rel="nofollow noopener">working hours</a> and <a href="https://support.google.com/calendar/answer/10738043" rel="nofollow noopener">Time Insights</a>. Written by the developer of Life Console, a free time-blocking planner.</p>

<p class="summary">Time blocking means giving each task its own slot on the clock. Google Calendar is free, already on most phones, and good at it: a task can sit on the grid with a start time and a length, next to your meetings. Five steps below, then the two things it won't do for you, and what to use if you need them. Steps 1 to 3 work on any Google account; <b>Focus time and Time Insights need a work or school account</b>.</p>

## 1. List the week's tasks, each with a length

Before you open the calendar, write down everything you want to do this week and how long each one will take. Round up; most tasks take longer than you think. Split anything over about two hours into pieces.

In Google Calendar, you can keep this list as **tasks**: Google's help says you can "enter the start date and how much time you plan to spend on the task", and a task with a date appears on the calendar. A task without a date stays in the Tasks list until you give it one.

## 2. Put in what can't move first

Meetings, classes, the commute, the school run. Anything that repeats goes in once as a **recurring event**, so next week starts with it already there. What's left between these is the time you actually have, and it's usually less than you hoped.

## 3. Give each task a block

In **Day** or **Week** view, click an empty slot and choose **Task** (or an event, if you prefer), then set its start and end time. For a task that's already on your list, open it and add a time. Some tips that make the grid readable:

- **Hardest work in your best hours.** For most people that's the morning, before messages pile up.
- **Colour by kind of work:** one colour for deep work, one for admin, one for personal. A glance at the week then shows the balance.
- **Group small tasks** (email, errands, calls) into one block instead of scattering them.
- **Leave gaps.** A day blocked solid from 9 to 6 breaks at the first overrun. Plan about 6 or 7 hours of work, not 8 or more.

## 4. Protect deep work with Focus time

On an eligible **work or school** account (Google lists Business Standard and Plus, Enterprise, Education and Nonprofits), create the block as **Focus time** from Day or Week view. It can **automatically decline meetings** during the block and **mute chat notifications**. On a personal Gmail account Focus time isn't available: make a normal event called *Focus* instead, and treat it as a meeting with yourself.

## 5. Review each evening

Look at today's blocks. Mark done tasks complete, and for each one that slipped, decide: move it to another slot, put it back on the list without a date, or delete it. Then glance at tomorrow. On a work or school account, **Time Insights** (on a computer) shows how the week's time split between focus time, one-to-ones and meetings.

## What Google Calendar won't do for you

Two things trip people up after a couple of weeks:

- **Unfinished blocks don't move on their own.** If a block slips, it stays in the past until you move it. Skip the evening review and the plan quietly falls apart.
- **It doesn't know when a day is too full.** You can put ten hours of tasks into a seven-hour day; nothing warns you until the evening.

If those don't bother you, Google Calendar is all you need. If they do, two kinds of app help:

- **Your day is mostly meetings and you want tasks on that calendar:** Sunsama or Akiflow put tasks onto your Google calendar, and Akiflow's AI can place them in free slots. Both are paid. Compare them in [the best time blocking apps →](/alternatives/time-blocking-apps)
- **Your day is mostly your own work:** a planner that starts from the task list. Life Console is one: free and open source, it gives each day a 7-hour budget that turns red when it's overfull, places untimed tasks into free gaps with **Auto time-box**, and walks you through what slipped each evening. It has no Google Calendar sync, so meetings go in as fixed blocks.

## Questions people ask

### How do I make a time block in Google Calendar?

Open Day or Week view, click the empty slot where the block should go, give it a title and choose **Task** or an event, then set the start and end time. To turn an existing task into a block, open it and add a time.

### How do I block off time so people can't book meetings over it?

On a work or school account, use **Focus time** and leave *Automatically decline meetings* on; it declines new and existing invitations during the block. You can also set **working hours** so colleagues see when you're available. Personal accounts don't have either option.

### Is time blocking in Google Calendar free?

Yes. Tasks, events, recurring events and colours are free on any Google account. Focus time, working hours and Time Insights come with eligible Google Workspace work or school accounts.

### Can Google Calendar time block my tasks automatically?

No: you place each block yourself. Apps that schedule tasks for you work alongside it (Akiflow's auto-schedule, for one), and Life Console's **Auto time-box** does it inside its own planner.
